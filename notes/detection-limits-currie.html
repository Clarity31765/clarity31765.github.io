Detection limits, part 1: Currie and the three limits

Where the modern idea of a detection limit comes from, and why “detected” and “detectable” are not the same thing.

By Oliver Lee, October 2026

Series
  1. Part 1 · Currie
  2. Part 2 · EPA MDL
  3. Part 3 · SEMI C10

Why start here

Almost every certificate of analysis in trace analysis reports a "detection limit" or "MDL". Most of the definitions in use today, in standards, regulations and instrument software, trace back to one paper.

Citation: L. A. Currie, "Limits for Qualitative Detection and Quantitative Determination: Application to Radiochemistry", Analytical Chemistry, 1968, 40, 586–593.

Note on the name: the author is Lloyd A. Currie (with -ie). The name is often misspelled as "Curry".

The problem in 1968

Currie did not invent the detection limit. Detection limits were already widely used, but there was no agreement on them:

  • The literature used many different formulas and terms.
  • The same words were used for two different questions: "can we see it?" and "can we measure it?".
  • Some common recipes had no sound statistical basis.

To show how bad this was, Currie applied eight common recipes to a single counting example. The answers differed by a factor of nearly 1000.

What he proposed: three limits

Currie split the problem into three limits, each with a clear statistical meaning.

Limit Meaning Well-known blank Paired blank and sample
LC, critical level Decision point: above this, call the result "detected" 1.64 σ₀ 2.33 σ₀
LD, detection limit The smallest true amount that will be detected with high probability 3.29 σ₀ 4.65 σ₀
LQ, determination limit The level where a result has an acceptable relative uncertainty (10% here) 10 σ₀ 14.1 σ₀

σ₀ is the standard deviation of the blank. The factors assume:

  • α = 0.05: the chance of calling a blank "detected" (false positive)
  • β = 0.05: the chance of missing a sample truly at LD (false negative)
  • a standard deviation that does not change with concentration

The key idea: LC and LD are different

This is the part most often lost in practice.

  • LC is about the measured result. It is a decision rule. It controls false positives only.
  • LD is about the true amount in the sample. It controls false negatives as well. With α = β = 0.05 it is about twice LC (3.29 vs 1.64).

A consequence follows directly from the definitions: if a sample truly contains exactly LC, its measured results scatter around LC, so it will be called "detected" only about half the time.

And neither limit means the number is a good measurement. Quantitative results start around LQ, which is about three times LD (10 vs 3.29).

The idea in one picture

How to read it

  • Grey curve: results you would get from a blank, a sample with nothing in it. They scatter around zero with standard deviation σ₀.
  • Teal curve: results from a sample whose true amount is exactly LD. They scatter around LD with the same σ₀.
  • Red dashed line, LC = 1.64σ₀: the decision rule. Any result to the right is reported as "detected".
  • Red shaded tail (α = 5%): blank results that land right of LC. These are false positives.
  • Orange shaded tail (β = 5%): results from the LD sample that land left of LC. These are missed detections.

How LD is calculated

LD is placed so that the two shaded tails are both 5%. Each tail sits 1.645σ₀ from its curve's center, so:

  • LC = 1.645σ₀ (the blank curve's 95th percentile)
  • LD = LC + 1.645σ₀ = 3.29σ₀

LQ sits far to the right at 10σ₀, where one standard deviation is 10% of the value. That is where a number becomes a measurement, not just a yes or no.

What came after

According to NIST, Currie's framework was taken up across measurement practice, international standards, regulation and software. It led to a harmonized ISO–IUPAC position on detection, decision and determination limits, now part of the IUPAC Compendium of Analytical Nomenclature.

The rest of this series looks at two definitions used in practice and compares them with Currie's:

  • Part 2: the US EPA MDL, which in its original form is a decision threshold like LC
  • Part 3: SEMI C10, used for semiconductor process chemicals, which builds the MDL from a calibration curve

Practical takeaways

  1. "Not detected" is not "zero". A sample below LC can still contain up to about LD without being reliably seen.
  2. "Detected" is not "measured". Between LC and LQ, report with care, or report as detected but not quantified.
  3. Always say which limit you mean. "Detection limit" alone could be LC or LD, and they differ by a factor of two.
