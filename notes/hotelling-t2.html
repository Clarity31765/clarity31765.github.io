Hotelling’s T², from distance to control limit

Two results can each sit inside ±3σ and still be a combination the process never makes. Hotelling’s T² is built to catch exactly that.

By Oliver Lee, November 2026

The problem

When two quality characteristics move together, for example two impurities that come from the same source, checking each one on its own chart misses something. A result can be normal for variable 1, normal for variable 2, and still be a combination that does not happen when the process is healthy. To see it, you need one number that measures how far a point is from the centre of the joint distribution. This note builds that number in three steps.

Step 1: Euclidean distance, and why it is not enough

The obvious choice is the straight-line distance from the centre, the hypotenuse of a right triangle:

d² = (x₁ − μ₁)² + (x₂ − μ₂)²

It has two blind spots:

  1. Scale. It changes with the units. A variable reported in ppm will outweigh one reported in ppt simply because its numbers are bigger.
  2. Correlation. Every direction counts the same, so the limit is a circle. A point that moves along the usual correlation and a point that moves against it get the same distance.

The second point is the important one. Euclidean distance can tell you how far a point is, but not whether the combination is a normal one.

Step 2: Mahalanobis distance

P. C. Mahalanobis proposed this distance in 1936. In matrix form:

D² = (x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ)

For two variables it is easier to read. First standardize each variable, zi = (xi − μi) / σi, which removes the scale problem. Then:

D² = ( z₁² − 2ρ·z₁·z₂ + z₂² ) / (1 − ρ²)

The middle term, −2ρ·z₁·z₂, is where the correlation does its work. Take ρ > 0 (the two variables usually rise and fall together):

  • Same direction (z₁ and z₂ have the same sign): z₁z₂ > 0, so the term is subtracted and the distance gets smaller. This is a combination the process makes all the time.
  • Opposite directions: z₁z₂ < 0, the minus signs cancel, the term is added and the distance gets larger.

The factor 1/(1 − ρ²) makes this stronger as the correlation gets tighter. If the variables are uncorrelated and have unit variance, Σ is the identity matrix and D² is just the Euclidean distance again.

Step 3: Hotelling’s T²

In practice μ and Σ are not known, so they are estimated from a baseline: the sample mean vector x̄ and the sample covariance matrix S. Plug those in and you get Hotelling’s T² (Hotelling, 1947):

T² = (x − x̄)ᵀ S⁻¹ (x − x̄)

So T² is the squared Mahalanobis distance with estimated parameters. For subgroup means of size n, the same expression is multiplied by n.

All points with the same T² lie on an ellipse around the centre. The ellipse where T² equals the upper control limit is the familiar “rugby ball”. Its tilt comes from the correlation and its long and short axes from the variance in each direction.

Point B is the case this note is about. It is within ±3σ on both separate charts and inside the Euclidean circle, but its T² is 22.5 against a limit of 11.8. Only the ellipse catches it.

The ellipse can only be drawn for two variables. The working chart is a Shewhart-style chart: T² plotted over time against a single upper limit. A lower limit is usually not used, because a squared distance cannot be negative.

Setting the upper control limit

The limit depends on what you know, so there are three cases. Here p is the number of variables and m is the number of individual observations in the baseline.

SituationDistributionUCL (individual observations)
μ and Σ knownχ²χ²α, p
Phase I: limits from the baseline, applied to the same baselineBeta((m − 1)² / m) · B1−α/2; p/2, (m−p−1)/2
Phase II: limits from the baseline, applied to new dataF[p(m + 1)(m − 1) / (m² − mp)] · Fα; p, m−p

The Phase I and Phase II forms for individual observations are from Tracy, Young and Mason (1992), as given in the NIST handbook. The NIST Phase I form uses α/2 because it also states a lower limit.

Why three different distributions

Think of it as building a ruler and then measuring with it.

  • χ²: you have the answer key. The true mean and covariance are known, so the ruler is exact. In a plant this is rare. It is mainly the reference the other two approach.
  • Phase I, Beta: you measure the people who built the ruler. Each point being tested helped estimate x̄ and S, and pulled them a little toward itself. So it looks less extreme than it really is, and the limit is tighter. The purpose is to clean the baseline.
  • Phase II, F: you measure a newcomer with a finished ruler. The new point played no part in the estimate, but the ruler itself is only an estimate from m points and carries error. The limit is wider to allow for that. The (m + 1) term does this.

Things to watch in practice

  • A signal does not say which variable. T² is one number. To find the cause, decompose it, for example with the Mason–Tracy–Young (MYT) decomposition, which splits T² into a term for each variable plus conditional terms.
  • Do not use T² for everything. If a shift affects only one variable, that variable’s own chart is usually faster and easier to read. Keep the univariate charts alongside.
  • Redundant variables break it. If two variables are perfectly correlated, or one is the sum of others, S cannot be inverted. Remove the redundant variable.
  • It assumes multivariate normality. Trace results near the detection limit are often skewed or censored. Check the distribution, and consider a log transform, before trusting the limits.

Practical takeaways

  1. Euclidean distance measures how far. Mahalanobis distance also asks whether the combination is normal.
  2. T² is the squared Mahalanobis distance with estimated mean and covariance.
  3. Answer key: χ². Same data: Beta, tighter. New data: F, wider. With a large baseline, all three meet.
