Three detection limits for one instrument

A customer asks for your detection limit for iron in sulfuric acid. The honest first answer is “which one?”

By Oliver Lee, October 2026

The same ICP-MS, the same blanks and the same calibration standards can produce noticeably different “detection limits” depending on which definition you apply. That is not sloppiness; the definitions answer different questions. Lloyd Currie made the point bluntly in 1968: applying eight common textbook definitions to a single radioactivity measurement gave limits spread over a factor of nearly 1000.

This note walks through the three definitions I meet most often in semiconductor materials work: Currie’s framework, the EPA method detection limit, and the calibration-curve approach in SEMI C10.

1. Currie: two questions, two errors

Currie separated two questions that are easy to blur together:

  • Decision: given this measurement, do I say the analyte is present? The threshold is the critical level, LC. Setting it trades off the false-positive rate α.
  • Capability: how much analyte must truly be there before I will reliably detect it? That is the detection limit, LD, which also limits the false-negative rate β.

He added a third level for quantitation, the determination limit, LQ, the level at which the relative standard deviation falls to a stated value; with his kQ = 10 that is 10% RSD.

With α = β = 0.05, a known and constant standard deviation σ0 of the net blank signal, and a normal distribution, the levels reduce to simple multiples:

LC = 1.645 σ0    LD = 3.29 σ0    LQ = 10 σ0

The familiar “three sigma of the blank” rule sits close to LD in this picture, but it is usually applied without saying which error rates it assumes or whether σ0 is known or estimated from a handful of blanks. Those details move the answer.

2. EPA method detection limit (40 CFR 136, Appendix B, Revision 2)

The US EPA procedure, which grew out of work published by Glaser and co-authors in 1981, is the definition many environmental and contract labs know best. Revision 2, published in December 2016, defines the MDL as the minimum measured concentration that can be reported with 99% confidence that it is distinguishable from method-blank results.

The procedure in outline:

  • Analyse at least seven spiked samples and at least seven method blanks, prepared in at least three batches on three separate dates.
  • Spike at typically two to ten times the estimated detection limit.
  • Compute the spike-based value from the replicate standard deviation Ss:
MDLS = t(n−1, 0.99) × Ss    (t = 3.143 for n = 7)
  • Compute a blank-based value MDLb: not applicable if no blank gives a numerical result; the highest blank result if only some do; the blank mean plus t × Sb if all do.
  • Report the larger of MDLS and MDLb, and recalculate at least once a year from data collected over up to the last 24 months.

Two features matter for comparison. It covers the whole method, sample preparation included, not just the instrument. And Revision 2’s blank term means contamination in your reagents or labware now raises the reported MDL, which is exactly the effect a semiconductor customer worries about.

3. SEMI C10: an MDL from the calibration curve

SEMI C10, the Guide for Determination of Method Detection Limits, was first published in 1998; the current revision is C10-1109, reapproved in 2014. It is written for trace contaminants in process chemicals and gases and determines the MDL from a regression analysis of a calibration curve rather than from replicate spikes at one level.

Published discussions of the guide describe the idea as follows: fit the calibration line, build the prediction interval for a future measurement at zero concentration, and convert the upper limit of that interval back into a concentration (inverse prediction). C10 recommends weighted least squares, so that points with larger scatter at higher concentrations count for less.

Watch the weights. A worked example from JMP compared this weighted approach with ordinary least squares on the same data. Because the scatter at zero concentration was far smaller than elsewhere, the inverse-variance weights let the zero level dominate and the weighted estimate came out at 0.0014 against 9.6 from the unweighted fit. Before trusting a weighted MDL, plot the residuals and check that the variance model is reasonable at the low end.

I have summarised C10 from SEMI’s published abstract and from secondary discussions; the standard itself sets out the exact calculation and should be followed for any reported value.

5. What I do in practice

  1. Name the definition next to the number on any certificate, report or audit answer, with the matrix, isotope and preparation.
  2. Match the customer’s specification. If a spec cites SEMI C10, a replicate-spike MDL is not the same number, even when the lab’s SOP uses one.
  3. Never compare across definitions. Supplier A’s 3σ LOD and supplier B’s EPA-style MDL do not rank the two labs.
  4. Keep the blanks in the estimate. In ultra-trace work the blank, not the detector, usually sets the floor.
  5. Recalculate on a schedule and after any change to the instrument, reagents or clean-room conditions.
