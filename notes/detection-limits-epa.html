Detection limits, part 2: the EPA MDL, from 1981 to Revision 2

How a wastewater procedure became the most quoted “MDL”, what was wrong with it, and what the 2017 revision changed.

By Oliver Lee, October 2026

Series

Why it matters outside environmental labs

The EPA procedure is a US regulation for wastewater testing under the Clean Water Act. But its "7 replicates × t-value" recipe spread far beyond that, and many labs in other fields still calculate an "MDL" this way. Knowing where it came from, and what it does and does not tell you, helps when reading anyone's MDL.

Origin: a 1981 paper

The EPA MDL comes from a paper by EPA scientists:

J. A. Glaser, D. L. Foerst, G. D. McKee, S. A. Quave, W. L. Budde, "Trace analyses for wastewaters", Environmental Science & Technology, 1981, 15, 1426–1435.

The procedure was then written into US regulation as Appendix B to 40 CFR Part 136, published in the Federal Register in 1984 (49 FR 43430). The 2017 rule describes this original procedure as promulgated in 1983 and not revised since. Both dates refer to the same procedure.

The original MDL (Revision 1.11)

  • Defined as the minimum concentration that can be reported with 99% confidence that the true concentration is greater than zero.
  • At least 7 replicate spiked samples.
  • MDL = t(n−1, 0.99) × s, where s is the standard deviation of the replicates. For 7 replicates, t = 3.143.

Compared with Currie

Putting the two definitions side by side (see Part 1):

  • The original EPA MDL controls false positives only (99% one-sided). In Currie's terms it is a decision threshold, like LC.
  • It does not control false negatives. A sample truly at the MDL will be missed about half the time.
  • It is built from spiked replicates, not from blanks.

What went wrong in practice

EPA and state lab-certification bodies list several weaknesses:

  • "Zero" cannot really be measured, and analytes often appear in blanks. The old MDL ignored blanks.
  • Best-case numbers. Replicates could all be run on one day, often right after instrument maintenance.
  • One instrument for all. Labs often ran the MDL on their most sensitive instrument and applied it to the others.
  • Contamination now dominates. As instruments became more sensitive, background from the lab, consumables and equipment started to matter more than instrument noise.
  • Analytes often could not actually be measured at the MDL in a real sample.

The road to revision: the mercury method case

The weaknesses were not only academic. They came to a head in a legal challenge:

Date Event
8 Jun 1999 EPA promulgates Method 1631 Revision B for ultra-trace mercury in water (64 FR 30417)
1999 Industry groups challenge the rule: Alliance of Automobile Manufacturers et al. v. EPA, No. 99-1420 (D.C. Cir.)
19 Oct 2000 Settlement agreement. Among other terms, EPA agrees to assess its procedures for detection and quantitation limits and consider alternatives
12 Mar 2003 EPA publishes its assessment and proposes a revised MDL procedure plus a new quantitation level (ML)
8 Nov 2004 EPA withdraws the proposal; most commenters disfavored it. The existing MDL stays unchanged while EPA works with stakeholders
28 Aug 2017 Revision 2 is finalized (below)

So Revision 2 was not a direct result of the settlement. The settlement started a reassessment, the first proposal failed, and the version that finally passed came out of a later stakeholder process.

Revision 2 (2017)

The revision was suggested by The NELAC Institute and finalized in the Clean Water Act Methods Update Rule (Federal Register, 28 August 2017; effective 27 September 2017).

Revision 1.11 Revision 2
Definition 99% confidence the result is greater than zero Lowest measured result that can be reported with 99% confidence as distinguishable from method blanks
Spiked samples At least 7 At least 7, in at least 3 batches on 3 separate days
Method blanks Not used At least 7, giving a blank-based MDL (MDLb)
Reported MDL t × s of spikes The higher of MDLs (spikes) and MDLb (blanks)
Several instruments Not addressed Data pooled across instruments, at least 2 spikes and 2 blanks each
Ongoing checks None required Spikes every quarter; recalculate at least every 13 months
Outlier removal Not specified Statistical outlier removal not allowed; only documented gross failures

The blank-based MDL

  • If no blank gives a numerical result, MDLb does not apply.
  • If some blanks give numerical results, MDLb is the highest blank result (or the 99th percentile with 100 or more blanks).
  • If all blanks give numerical results, MDLb = blank mean + t(n−1, 0.99) × blank standard deviation (a negative mean is taken as zero).

What did not change

The calculation from spiked samples is the same. What changed is that the MDL now has to hold up against real blanks and real day-to-day variation, not one good day.

The calculation in one picture

Example data: 7 spiked samples (true value 1.0 µg/L) and 7 method blanks, all in µg/L. For a short illustration they are shown as 7 replicates; a real Revision 2 study spreads them over at least 3 days.

Replicate 1 2 3 4 5 6 7
Spiked 1.38 1.39 1.45 1.35 1.28 1.35 1.42
Blank 0.62 0.21 0.24 0.51 0.51 0.35 0.42

Step 1, from the spikes (teal): the spikes are tight, s = 0.055. MDLs = 3.143 × 0.055 = 0.173 µg/L. Under the old procedure, this would be the MDL.

Step 2, from the blanks (red): every blank gives a number, with mean 0.41 and s = 0.151. MDLb = 0.41 + 3.143 × 0.151 = 0.883 µg/L.

Step 3, report the higher: MDL = 0.883 µg/L, about 5 times the old value.

What the picture shows: the spikes alone suggest a method that can see 0.17 µg/L. But the blanks regularly read 0.2 to 0.6 µg/L, so a sample result of 0.3 µg/L could easily be nothing but blank. The old MDL hides that; the blank-based MDL exposes it.

The spike recovery here is about 137%, another hint that the blank is adding to every result.

Practical takeaways

  1. Ask which revision. An MDL from the old one-day procedure is likely lower than one from Revision 2.
  2. At ultra-trace levels, blanks set the limit. This is the main lesson of Revision 2, and it applies just as much to ppt-level work in semiconductor chemicals.
  3. The EPA MDL is still a decision threshold. For a limit that also controls missed detections, or for quantification, look at Currie's LD and LQ.
